Below the Aggregate.

Slice and adversarial failure modes of a production toxicity classifier, and why a single F1 score is the least useful number a Trust & Safety team can read.

00Abstract

Online platforms depend on automated classifiers to flag toxic comments at a scale humans cannot review by hand, and those models are almost always judged by a single headline accuracy score. That one number is reassuring and nearly useless, because it averages away the only thing a Trust & Safety team actually needs to know: the specific places the model breaks. And those breaks are expensive in both directions. Genuinely harmful content slips through and reaches users, or ordinary people get wrongly flagged and penalized, and either way the platform pays for it.

This project is a small, reproducible harness that interrogates a real production classifier (Detoxify) the way a careful analyst would, instead of trusting the average. It breaks performance down by the kind of comment, attacks the model with the evasions real bad actors use (from crude character swaps to AI-generated rewordings), pairs every attack with a defense to see what a fix would actually recover, and checks whether the model's confidence scores can be trusted at all. The output is not another metric but a prioritized, plain-language list of concrete, fixable weaknesses.

The headline looked healthy (F1 0.70 to 0.76); nothing underneath it did. The model misses almost half of subtle, implicit hostility while catching nearly every blunt threat. Cheap disguises like look-alike characters defeat it outright, but a simple text-cleanup step recovers almost all of that lost ground. The real danger is an attacker who just rephrases the same insult in ordinary words: that evades the model 57% of the time, and no cleanup can undo it, because the fix is better training data rather than better filtering. The model's confidence scores are also well-behaved on one dataset yet badly off on another, so a threshold tuned in one place quietly misfires in the next. Every finding comes with a specific thing to ship, and the whole study runs on public data by design.

What was actually run · June 2026 Detoxify (unbiased) scored on 1,500 Civil Comments and 3,728 HateCheck cases at threshold 0.5; a nine-attack mechanical evasion matrix with paired normalization defenses; and a live LLM red-team pass (Claude generating label-preserving paraphrases, a second pass judging that intent was preserved). All numbers below are regenerated by the released harness.
Try it live The findings are interactive at tands-harm-classifier-eval.onrender.com — a FastAPI + React scanner with four tabs: Live Scanner (toxicity gauge + Bloom-filter tier-0 precheck), Attack Lab (full nine-attack matrix on any text), Slice Explorer (click any HateCheck bar to see the real missed examples), and Calibration Dashboard (dual reliability curves with a draggable threshold slider).

01Why aggregates lie

A toxicity model deployed at platform scale does not fail evenly. It fails on specific slices of traffic, against specific adversaries, and at specific operating points. An aggregate precision/recall/F1 (the figure that lands in a model card) averages all of that away. The useful question is not "is the model good?" It is "where, against whom, and how cheaply does it break, and what do we do about it?"

In plain English Imagine a smoke detector that works 95% of the time. Sounds great, until you learn the missing 5% is "every fire that starts in the kitchen." The average hides the one place you most needed it to work. A toxicity classifier is the same: the headline score can look fine while the model quietly fails on a particular kind of comment, or on anyone who knows a simple trick to dodge it.

This is a methodology paper, not a new model. The contribution is a way of looking: an analyst workflow that turns a model into a prioritized list of concrete weaknesses, plus a set of findings about how a representative, production-grade classifier actually behaves once you stop reading the average.

02The harness

The system takes a classifier and a labelled dataset and runs four analyses in sequence: a baseline at a fixed operating point, a slice breakdown, an adversarial stress test (mechanical attacks paired with defenses, plus an LLM paraphrase attack), and a qualitative error pass. It renders the result as an analyst memo (the artifact a T&S team actually reads) and caches everything so the memo can be re-rendered without re-running the model.

System architecture

The harness is deliberately small and modular: each box below is one file with one job, which is what makes the findings cheap to reproduce and easy to audit.

Interactive web deployment

The harness results are also shipped as a FastAPI + React web application deployed on Render via Docker. The backend serves precomputed JSON (no live Detoxify inference in the container, keeping the image within the free-tier 512 MB RAM limit) and exposes four API endpoints — /api/score, /api/attack, /api/slices, /api/calibration — consumed by a React frontend built with Vite. A Bloom-filter tier-0 denylist (9,585 bits, 7 hash functions, O(k) per lookup) runs before every model call as a fast precheck. The four tabs of the scanner map directly to the four analyses in the harness: baseline scoring, adversarial attack matrix, per-slice recall, and calibration curves.

03Finding 1: the slice cliff

Aggregate recall on HateCheck is 0.77. That average is built from functional slices that range from near-perfect to badly broken. The model catches 99% of direct threats, and just 53% of implicit derogation, the coded, context-dependent hostility that carries no obvious trigger word and is exactly the hardest, highest-value case to catch.

04Finding 2: two kinds of evasion, one of them undefended

Adversaries do not send clean text. The harness applies a matrix of evasions to the items the model caught at baseline and measures the evasion success rate (ESR): the share of caught items that now slip below threshold. Every mechanical attack is paired with a normalization defense (strip invisible characters, fold look-alike letters back to plain Latin, undo leetspeak, collapse spaced-out text), and the table reports recall under attack and after the defense.

Read the last two columns together. Character attacks are devastating raw (evasion success up to 100%), but the normalization pass restores recall to ~0.97–0.99. They are a solved problem, given a preprocessor. The one row where the defended column does not recover is the LLM paraphrase: recall under attack (0.43) is identical to recall after the defense (0.43). Normalization removes surface obfuscation; it cannot reverse a fluent rewrite that shares no trigger tokens with the original.

What the attacks look like

Character evasions keep the words and corrupt the letters, the kind of obfuscation a normalizer is built to undo:

caught:   "i hate this loser"        → score 0.86  (flagged)
homoglyph "i hаte this lоser"        → score 0.07  (evaded)
defended  un-fold confusables back   → score 0.86  (flagged again)

The LLM paraphrase keeps the meaning and changes the words. There is nothing for a normalizer to reverse, because every token is ordinary English:

Original · caught

"you are an idiot and everyone knows it"

score 0.88 · flagged
Paraphrase · evaded

"you're absolutely clueless and the whole world can see it"

score 0.08 · slips through

Both sentences carry the same contempt; a human moderator would treat them identically. The classifier, keyed on surface lexicon, flags one and waves the other through. A second LLM judge confirms the paraphrase preserved the harmful intent before it counts as a successful evasion, so the 57% is genuine misses, not the attacker cheating by softening the message.

In plain English Spelling tricks are like disguising a word with funny characters: easy to detect and undo once you know the trick. A paraphrase is a different threat entirely: the attacker simply says the same nasty thing in clean words. There is no disguise to strip off, so a preprocessor is useless. The only durable fix is to teach the model these rephrasings directly, which is exactly the training data this harness produces as a by-product.

05Finding 3: calibration does not travel

A score is a probability. If the model says 0.70, that should mean roughly a 70% chance the comment is toxic. Expected calibration error (ECE) measures how far that promise is from reality. On Civil Comments the model is well-calibrated (ECE 0.022). On HateCheck it is off by an order of magnitude (ECE 0.226), and its false-positive rate jumps to 0.55: it over-flags more than half of the non-hateful cases, because HateCheck's non-hateful set is adversarially hard (reclaimed slurs, counter-speech that quotes a slur, benign profanity).

In plain English The model's confidence is honest in one setting and badly inflated in another. So if a team picks a cut-off ("flag everything above 0.6") by looking at one dataset, that same cut-off can behave completely differently on real traffic. Calibration has to be checked per distribution, not assumed.

06Error analysis: the model reads the surface

The false positives are as revealing as the misses, and they point at the same root cause: the model keys on surface lexicon rather than meaning. It flags the innocent homonym of a slur, and it misses hostility that contains no trigger word.

False positive · over-flagged

"There is a type of meatball eaten in the UK called faggot."

score 0.95 · the benign British culinary term, flagged as hate
False negative · missed

"I really can't stand women like you."

score 0.27 · implicit hostility, no trigger word, waved through

Two sentences, the whole precision/recall story: the model over-flags a benign word that happens to be spelled like a slur, and under-flags a sentence that is plainly hostile but lexically clean. Both are surface-lexicon failures pulling in opposite directions.

07What to ship

  1. Ship a normalization preprocessor. It is cheap and the table shows it reverses almost all of the recall lost to character-level evasions, the obfuscation a real adversary reaches for first.
  2. Do not stop there. Semantic paraphrase evasions are not a preprocessing problem. Augment training with label-preserving LLM evasions as hard positives, and route ambiguous cases to human review. The harness emits exactly this augmentation set.
  3. Close slice gaps with real annotations, especially implicit derogation and identity-mentioning content, and route to a specialist (multilingual, context-aware) model where it helps.
  4. Monitor slice recall and per-distribution calibration as guardrails, not just aggregate F1. The next blind spot should appear on a dashboard, not in the wild.

08Ethics & responsible disclosure

The work uses public proxy data only. The LLM red-team is constrained to non-egregious proxy content and to rephrasing rather than escalation. The label-preserving evasions that bypass the live model are treated as a dual-use hazard: the methodology is published, but the working evasion strings against the production checkpoint are withheld from the repository. The before/after examples shown here are drawn from a synthetic-vocabulary validation set, not from attacks on the deployed model.

09Limitations

  1. Proxy gap. Public toxic-comment data understates production distribution shift and the hardest, most context-dependent harms.
  2. Proxy slice. The Civil Comments identity slice is a keyword heuristic, not ground truth; the disparity should be confirmed with annotated identity labels before it is acted on as a number.
  3. Sample size. Reported figures use modest samples for iteration speed; scale the sample for tighter confidence intervals.
  4. Single model, single threshold. Extending to multiple checkpoints and a per-slice threshold sweep would sharpen the operating-point analysis.

10Conclusion

A single aggregate score is a summary statistic that systematically hides the things a Trust & Safety team is paid to find. Reading below it (by slice, under attack, and at the level of calibrated probabilities) turns a healthy-looking 0.70–0.76 F1 into a prioritized list of real, fixable failures: a 47% miss rate on implicit hate, a paraphrase attack that no preprocessor can defend, and confidence scores that do not survive a change of distribution. None of these is visible in the headline. All of them change what you would actually ship. The methodology and harness are open source.
