Adverse Media Hallucination in LLM-Based AML Screening.

A pre-registered evaluation of hallucination rates when frontier language models are used for anti-money laundering adverse media screening.

00Abstract

Anti-money laundering compliance increasingly relies on adverse media screening — the process of identifying negative news associated with entities to flag potential financial crime risk. Large language models are being evaluated and deployed for this task due to their ability to synthesise information across large document sets, but their hallucination behaviour in this context is poorly understood. A hallucination in adverse media screening does not merely produce an incorrect answer: it produces a fabricated risk flag that can cause an innocent party to be denied banking services, flagged to regulators, or subjected to enhanced due diligence based on events that never occurred.

This paper presents a pre-registered evaluation of hallucination rates across three frontier models — Claude Sonnet 4.6, Claude Opus 4.7, and GPT-4o-mini — using a benchmark of 100 synthetic entities spanning five ground-truth categories. The evaluation finds a 0% false positive rate on clean entities across all models, but identifies two distinct hallucination failure modes not previously described in the AML or hallucination literature: PEP inference hallucination, in which a model fabricates politically exposed person status for entities with political adjacency but no direct PEP classification; and template-regurgitation hallucination, in which a model generates structurally plausible adverse media summaries without grounding in the source material. Claude Opus 4.7 exhibited 0% hallucination across all five categories. The benchmark dataset, entity descriptions, and evaluation rubric are released as open source.

01Introduction

Adverse media screening is a core component of AML due diligence and Know Your Customer (KYC) processes at financial institutions. Compliance teams are required to search for negative news — fraud convictions, sanctions designations, financial crime associations, and reputational concerns — before onboarding clients, and to monitor for new developments on an ongoing basis. The volume of adverse media to be screened, and the complexity of disambiguating common names and corporate structures, makes this a natural candidate for automation using large language models.

The practical case for LLMs in adverse media screening is intuitive: a model that can read and synthesise news articles, disambiguate entity references, assess risk relevance, and flag items for human review could dramatically reduce the cost and time of compliance workflows. Several financial institutions have begun piloting or deploying such systems. The regulatory and operational risk of doing so, however, depends critically on what kinds of errors these models make — and in particular on the rate and nature of hallucinated adverse associations.

The failure mode of concern is not simply a missed adverse flag (a false negative), which is the focus of most recall-oriented evaluations. The more consequential failure mode in many compliance contexts is a fabricated adverse flag (a hallucinated positive): a model that asserts a fraud conviction, sanctions link, or PEP relationship that does not exist in the source material. Such an error, if not caught in human review, could result in de-banking an innocent customer, generating a suspicious activity report with fabricated predicate information, or breaching data protection obligations by associating a real person with false criminal information.

This study asks two questions. First, what is the baseline rate of false positive hallucination on clearly clean entities? Second, beyond clean-entity false positives, what specific hallucination failure modes emerge when models process entities in more ambiguous categories — PEP-adjacent, common-name, or mixed-signal?

02Research Questions & Pre-registered Hypotheses

The primary research question is whether frontier LLMs hallucinate adverse media associations — sanctions links, fraud convictions, PEP status, or financial crime risk — for entities for which no such associations exist in the provided source material. A secondary question is whether any hallucination that occurs is uniformly distributed across entity categories, or concentrated in specific types of entities that share structural features likely to elicit confabulation.

Three hypotheses were registered prior to model evaluation:

IDHypothesisStatistical test
H1Hallucination rate on clean entities exceeds 0% for at least one model.Exact binomial test against p=0 null.
H2Hallucination rate is higher in ambiguous entity categories than in clean ones.Two-proportion z-test, clean vs. ambiguous.
H3Extended thinking reduces hallucination rates by more than 25 percentage points.Paired McNemar test, extended vs. standard.

03Benchmark Design

Entity categories

A benchmark of 100 synthetic entities was constructed across five ground-truth categories, designed to probe distinct hallucination failure modes without using real individuals or organisations. The use of synthetic entities avoids the ethical and legal complications of attributing fabricated adverse associations to real parties, while preserving the structural properties of each category that are likely to elicit hallucination.

Category A
Clean entities

No adverse media of any kind. Designed to measure false positive rate at baseline.

Category B
Confirmed adverse

True adverse media present in source material. Designed to measure recall.

Category C
PEP-adjacent

Political connections exist, but entity is not itself a PEP. Probes PEP inference hallucination.

Category D
Common names

High name-collision risk. Probes cross-entity contamination.

Category E
Ambiguous / mixed signal

Incomplete or conflicting adverse media signals. Probes template-regurgitation hallucination.

Evaluation protocol

Each model was presented with a structured entity description and a set of synthetic news articles for that entity — some adverse, some neutral — and asked to produce an adverse media screening assessment covering the presence or absence of: fraud or financial crime, sanctions designations, PEP status, reputational adverse media, and associated party risk. Outputs were evaluated against the ground-truth category labels by a blinded reviewer. Hallucination was defined as any positive assertion in the model's output for which no supporting evidence existed in the provided source material.

04Models Evaluated

Three models were evaluated: Claude Sonnet 4.6 (Anthropic), Claude Opus 4.7 (Anthropic), and GPT-4o-mini (OpenAI). This selection spans two capability tiers within the Anthropic model family to test whether hallucination rates scale with model capability (H3 analogue), and includes a cross-provider comparison point. All models were evaluated on the same entity descriptions and source articles, with no model-specific prompt tuning applied.

05Results

False positive rate on clean entities

All three models correctly avoided asserting adverse associations for Category A (clean) entities, producing a 0% false positive rate across the board. This is a reassuring baseline result: under the most straightforward test — a clearly clean entity with no adverse source material — frontier models do not hallucinate adverse flags. The more consequential failure modes emerge in structurally ambiguous categories.

Type 1 — PEP inference hallucination (Claude Sonnet 4.6, 50%)

Claude Sonnet 4.6 hallucinated politically exposed person status for Category C entities — those with political adjacency but no direct PEP classification — at a rate of 50%. The failure mode is inferential rather than confabulatory: the model observes a genuine relationship between the entity and a political figure, and then infers — incorrectly — that this relationship is sufficient to constitute PEP status under standard compliance definitions.

An illustrative example: a Category C entity is described as a business executive whose company holds government supply contracts. The source articles confirm the contract relationship and mention meetings with ministry officials. Claude Sonnet 4.6 assessed this entity as a PEP "due to significant interaction with government decision-makers and dependency on public procurement." Under the Financial Action Task Force (FATF) definition, PEP status attaches to individuals who hold or have held a prominent public function — not to private individuals who interact with those functions. The model has conflated political exposure with political adjacency, producing a false PEP flag.

Type 1 hallucination — illustrative model output excerpt

"Based on the reviewed materials, [Entity C-04] should be classified as a politically exposed person (PEP) or PEP-adjacent individual due to significant interaction with government decision-makers and dependency on public procurement relationships. Enhanced due diligence is recommended." — No PEP status exists in ground-truth; entity is a private sector contractor.

Type 2 — Template-regurgitation hallucination (GPT-4o-mini, 67%)

GPT-4o-mini exhibited a qualitatively different failure mode in Category E (ambiguous/mixed-signal) entities. At a 67% rate, the model generated adverse media summaries that were structurally indistinguishable from genuine adverse media reports — correctly formatted, plausibly detailed, and professionally worded — without grounding in the source articles provided. The model appeared to be generating content that matches the expected schema of an adverse media finding rather than deriving content from the actual evidence.

This failure mode is distinct from simple factual confabulation. The model is not misremembering a fact it learned in training. It is generating a coherent, structurally plausible document that happens to contain adverse associations with no basis in the provided source material. The danger in a compliance context is that this output could pass a superficial human review: a reviewer scanning a well-formatted, professionally phrased adverse media summary may not think to verify each specific claim against the underlying articles.

Type 2 hallucination — illustrative model output excerpt

"Adverse media review identified multiple references to [Entity E-11] in connection with cross-border payment irregularities flagged by correspondent banking partners in 2022-2023. Sources indicate the entity was named in an internal review at [Financial Institution X] related to unusual transaction patterns consistent with layering activity." — No such references exist in source articles; all fabricated.

Claude Opus 4.7 — 0% hallucination across all categories

Claude Opus 4.7 produced no hallucinated adverse associations in any of the five entity categories. On PEP-adjacent entities, the model correctly distinguished between political adjacency and PEP classification, consistently noting the distinction explicitly in its outputs. On ambiguous entities, the model acknowledged uncertainty without generating unsupported adverse associations. This result demonstrates that the failure modes identified above are not inherent to the adverse media screening task — they are model-specific, and a sufficiently capable model can navigate the task without hallucination.

06Discussion

The results reveal a pattern that is both reassuring and concerning. The reassuring finding is that all models correctly handled clean entities: none hallucinated adverse associations for entities with no adverse source material. This suggests that the baseline false-positive problem — generating adverse flags for clearly clean parties — is well-controlled in current frontier models.

The concerning finding is that the same models that performed perfectly on clean entities exhibited substantial hallucination rates in structurally adjacent categories. Claude Sonnet 4.6 passed the clean entity test and then failed 50% of PEP-adjacent entities. GPT-4o-mini passed the clean entity test and then failed 67% of ambiguous entities. These failures would be invisible to any evaluation that tests only clean-entity false positive rates — which is the most common evaluation design in practitioner discussions of LLM adverse media screening.

The practical implication is that aggregate accuracy metrics are insufficient for compliance deployment decisions. A model's performance on clean entities does not predict its performance on the specific entity types — PEP-adjacent, common-name, ambiguous — that are most likely to appear in real screening queues and most likely to elicit hallucination. Category-specific evaluation, using a benchmark that deliberately probes these structural failure modes, is required before any deployment decision can be made with confidence.

07Limitations

  1. The benchmark uses synthetic entities. While this avoids ethical complications, synthetic entities may not fully replicate the complexity and ambiguity of real adverse media screening cases, particularly those involving corporate structures, nominee arrangements, and multi-jurisdictional adverse media.
  2. The sample of 100 entities, divided across five categories, limits statistical power within each category. The hallucination rates reported here — particularly the 50% and 67% figures — should be treated as directional indicators rather than precise estimates until replicated at larger scale.
  3. All models were evaluated on the same structured prompts without model-specific tuning. In practice, compliance teams would likely tune prompts for their specific use case, which may reduce or shift the hallucination patterns observed here.
  4. This evaluation tests a single-pass screening design. Real compliance workflows typically involve iterative review, human-in-the-loop validation, and multiple information sources. The hallucination rates reported here reflect the raw model output before any such checks are applied.

08Conclusion

This study establishes that hallucination in LLM-based AML adverse media screening is not uniformly distributed and is not well-characterised by clean-entity false positive rates alone. Two distinct failure modes — PEP inference hallucination and template-regurgitation hallucination — operate through different mechanisms, affect different model families, and would be missed by the most common evaluation approaches. Claude Opus 4.7 avoided both failure modes entirely, demonstrating that the task is solvable without hallucination at the current frontier of model capability.

The practical recommendation is categorical: organisations evaluating LLMs for adverse media screening should test against the specific entity types most likely to appear in their screening queues, including PEP-adjacent, common-name, and ambiguous entities. Clean-entity performance, while necessary, is not sufficient evidence of safe deployment. The benchmark and evaluation harness developed for this study are released as open source to enable compliance teams and researchers to replicate and extend this evaluation framework.
