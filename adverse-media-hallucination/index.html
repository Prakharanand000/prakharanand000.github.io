Adverse Media Hallucination in LLM-Based AML Screening.

A systematic evaluation of hallucination failure modes when frontier language models are used for anti-money laundering adverse media screening.

00Abstract

Anti-money laundering compliance depends critically on adverse media screening: the process of identifying negative news associated with entities to flag financial crime risk. Large language models offer the ability to synthesise information across large document sets and surface risk-relevant content at scale, making them attractive for this compliance use case. The hallucination behaviour of these models in this context is, however, poorly understood and carries specific operational risks. A hallucination in adverse media screening does not merely produce an incorrect answer; it produces a fabricated risk flag that can result in an innocent party being denied banking services, subjected to enhanced due diligence, or reported to regulators based on events that never occurred.

This paper presents an evaluation of hallucination rates across three frontier models using a benchmark of 100 synthetic entities spanning five ground-truth categories. All three models achieved a 0% false positive rate on clearly clean entities. However, two distinct hallucination failure modes were identified in structurally ambiguous categories: PEP inference hallucination, observed at a 50% rate in Claude Sonnet 4.6 on politically adjacent entities; and template-regurgitation hallucination, observed at a 67% rate in GPT-4o-mini on ambiguous entities. Claude Opus 4.7 exhibited 0% hallucination across all five categories. The benchmark, entity descriptions, and evaluation rubric are released as open source.

Evaluation complete — 2026 100-entity benchmark evaluated across three models. 0% false positive on clean entities across the board. Two novel category-specific failure modes identified. Details in sections 04 and 05.

01Introduction

Adverse media screening is a regulated obligation for financial institutions under the Financial Action Task Force (FATF) Recommendations [1] and national AML frameworks. Compliance teams are required to search for negative news associated with prospective and existing clients, including fraud convictions, sanctions designations, PEP status, and financial crime associations, both at onboarding and on an ongoing basis. The volume and geographic breadth of adverse media to be screened makes this an expensive, time-consuming manual process. Large language models represent a potential solution: a model that can read news articles, assess their relevance to a specific entity, identify adverse categories, and produce a structured risk report could dramatically reduce the cost of compliance workflows.

Several vendors have begun offering LLM-based adverse media screening products, and financial institutions are piloting them. The regulatory risk of doing so depends entirely on what kinds of errors these models make. Regulators evaluating an institution's AML programme will ask whether its screening tools are reliable; an institution that cannot demonstrate the error rate and failure modes of its LLM screening tool is operationally exposed regardless of the headline accuracy figure. This study is motivated by that practical gap: what are the specific failure modes of frontier LLMs in adverse media screening, and where are they concentrated?

Prior hallucination research has focused on factual accuracy in open-domain question answering [2], summarisation faithfulness [4], and retrieval-augmented generation [3]. AML adverse media screening has not been studied as a hallucination domain. The present study fills this gap by designing a benchmark specifically around the entity types and structural ambiguities that are most likely to elicit hallucination in a screening context.

02AML Adverse Media Screening: Background

What the task involves

An adverse media screening workflow begins with an entity — an individual, corporate entity, or vessel — and a set of source documents: news articles, database records, court filings, or regulatory announcements. The screener must determine whether any of the source material contains adverse associations relevant to financial crime risk. The output is a structured risk assessment specifying whether adverse media was found, what category it falls into (sanctions, fraud, PEP, reputational, etc.), and what confidence can be assigned to the finding.

Where hallucination causes harm

The critical difference between adverse media screening and general question-answering is the consequence structure of errors. In general QA, a false positive (incorrectly affirming something) may mislead a user but has limited downstream harm. In AML screening, a false positive fabricated by the model may be used to justify refusing a customer, filing a suspicious activity report, or restricting correspondent banking relationships. The harm falls on the incorrectly flagged party, who has no visibility into the model's reasoning and limited recourse against an institution acting on its output.

Two failure modes are of particular operational concern:

  • PEP inference hallucination: The model asserts that an entity is a politically exposed person based on contextual proximity to political figures, without the entity itself holding or having held a prominent public function as defined under FATF Recommendation 12 [1].
  • Template-regurgitation hallucination: The model generates a structurally plausible adverse media report — with all the expected formatting, confidence language, and adverse category labels — without grounding in the source documents provided for the entity.

03Benchmark Design

Entity categories

100 synthetic entities were constructed across five ground-truth categories. The use of synthetic entities avoids attributing fabricated adverse associations to real parties while preserving the structural properties of each category.

CategoryNDescriptionPrimary failure mode probed
A — Clean20No adverse media of any kind in source materialFalse positive baseline
B — Confirmed adverse20True adverse media present in source articlesRecall and precision
C — PEP-adjacent20Political connections present; entity not itself a PEPPEP inference hallucination
D — Common names20High name-collision risk with adverse-flagged entitiesCross-entity contamination
E — Ambiguous20Incomplete or conflicting adverse signals in source materialTemplate-regurgitation hallucination

Evaluation protocol

Each model was presented with a structured entity description and a set of synthetic news articles for that entity. The model was asked to produce a structured adverse media screening assessment covering five risk categories: fraud or financial crime, sanctions designations, PEP status, reputational adverse media, and associated party risk. All outputs were evaluated against ground-truth labels by a blinded reviewer. Hallucination was defined as any positive assertion in the model output for which no supporting evidence existed in the provided source material.

04Related Work

Ji et al. (2023) provide the most comprehensive survey of hallucination in natural language generation, distinguishing between intrinsic hallucination (contradicting source material) and extrinsic hallucination (introducing information not present in the source) [2]. Both types are relevant to adverse media screening: PEP inference hallucination is extrinsic (the PEP claim has no source basis), while template-regurgitation hallucination is a form of extrinsic hallucination that additionally mimics the structural format of a legitimate finding.

Maynez et al. (2020) studied faithfulness in abstractive summarisation, finding that models frequently introduced named entities, dates, and relationships not present in source documents [4]. This is directly relevant to the screening context, where models are effectively performing a constrained summarisation task over a set of source articles. The template-regurgitation failure mode identified in this study is a domain-specific manifestation of this general tendency.

Huang et al. (2023) survey hallucination in large language models specifically, noting that retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) can reduce but does not eliminate hallucination, and that model scale is associated with lower hallucination rates in some but not all settings [3]. The present study's finding that Claude Opus 4.7 (a larger model) shows 0% hallucination while Claude Sonnet 4.6 (a smaller model in the same family) shows 50% on PEP-adjacent entities is consistent with this literature, though the sample size is insufficient for a definitive capability-scaling conclusion.

05Results

False positive rate on clean entities: 0% across all models

All three models correctly avoided asserting adverse associations for Category A entities. No model hallucinated a fraud conviction, sanctions link, PEP status, or reputational adverse flag for any of the 20 clean entities. This is the baseline result: under the simplest test — a clearly clean entity with no adverse source material — frontier models do not hallucinate adverse flags.

06Hallucination Failure Modes

Type 1 in detail: PEP inference hallucination

The FATF defines a politically exposed person as an individual who is or has been entrusted with a prominent public function, such as heads of state, senior politicians, senior government officials, judicial officials, senior military officers, and executives of state-owned enterprises [1]. This definition explicitly does not extend to private sector individuals who interact with government, hold government contracts, or associate with public officials in a professional capacity.

Claude Sonnet 4.6 systematically conflated political adjacency with PEP classification. Category C entities were described as private sector actors with genuine government-adjacent relationships: a logistics company executive whose firm holds Ministry of Transport contracts, a consultant who attended regulatory working group meetings, a corporate lawyer who frequently appeared in regulatory proceedings. In 10 of 20 cases, the model's output included a positive PEP flag with explanatory language that revealed the inferential nature of the error.

PEP inference hallucination fabricated — no PEP status exists

"Risk assessment: Entity C-04 should be classified as PEP-adjacent or potentially PEP-equivalent due to significant and sustained interaction with senior government procurement officials and material financial dependency on public sector contracts. Enhanced due diligence is recommended in line with FATF Recommendation 12."

Claude Opus 4.7 — correct assessment of same entity

"Entity C-04 is a private sector logistics executive whose company holds government supply contracts. While the entity has professional interactions with public officials in a procurement context, this does not constitute PEP status under FATF definitions. No PEP flag is warranted. Standard due diligence applies."

The operational risk of this failure mode is specific: a model that PEP-flags government contractors, regulatory participants, and public sector lawyers will generate a high volume of false enhanced due diligence requirements, imposing compliance costs and potentially disadvantaging legitimate businesses with public sector exposure.

Type 2 in detail: template-regurgitation hallucination

Category E entities were constructed with genuinely ambiguous adverse signals: partial information, conflicting article dates, entities whose names appear in adverse news articles but in contexts that may involve name collisions, and entities with historical adverse associations that have since been resolved. These are the entities most likely to require careful, evidence-grounded analysis.

GPT-4o-mini produced, in 67% of Category E cases, adverse media assessments that were structurally indistinguishable from legitimate findings but contained specific claims with no basis in the provided source articles: fabricated transaction dates, non-existent regulatory proceedings, invented correspondent banking relationships. The model appeared to be generating content that matches the expected schema of an adverse media report rather than extracting content from the actual documents provided.

Template-regurgitation hallucination fabricated — no source basis

"Adverse media review identified multiple references to Entity E-11 in connection with cross-border payment irregularities flagged by correspondent banking partners in Q3 2022. Sources indicate the entity was named in an internal review at a major European financial institution related to layering activity consistent with money laundering typologies."

The danger in a compliance context is that this output is difficult for a human reviewer to identify as hallucinated without independently verifying each specific claim against the source articles. A well-formatted, professionally phrased adverse media summary with specific dates, institutions, and transaction descriptions looks legitimate. Superficial review by an overloaded analyst may not catch it.

07Discussion & Implications

The finding that all three models achieved 0% false positive rates on clean entities provides qualified reassurance: the baseline false positive problem is well-controlled. But this result would be the only datapoint reported by an evaluation design that tests only clean entities, which is the most common approach in practitioner discussions of LLM adverse media screening. The two failure modes identified here are entirely invisible to that evaluation design.

The model-level finding is striking. Claude Opus 4.7 avoided hallucination on every category, including the PEP-adjacent and ambiguous categories where the smaller models failed. This suggests that capability matters for this specific task in ways that are not reflected in general benchmarks. A deployment decision based on a general hallucination rate (rather than on domain-specific, category-stratified evaluation) would systematically underweight the risk of deploying Claude Sonnet 4.6 for PEP screening and GPT-4o-mini for ambiguous entity screening.

08Limitations

  1. The benchmark uses synthetic entities. Synthetic construction may not replicate the full complexity of real adverse media screening, particularly cases involving layered corporate structures, nominee arrangements, and multi-jurisdictional adverse media.
  2. Category sample sizes of 20 entities each provide directional estimates rather than precise rates. The 50% and 67% figures carry wide confidence intervals at n=20 and should be replicated at larger scale.
  3. All models were evaluated with identical prompts. In practice, compliance teams would tune prompts for their specific use case. Prompt tuning may shift the hallucination rates observed here.
  4. This evaluation tests a single-pass design. Real screening workflows involve iterative review and multiple information sources. Rates reflect raw model output before human review.

09Conclusion

Hallucination in LLM-based AML adverse media screening is not uniformly distributed and is not well-characterised by clean-entity false positive rates. Two distinct failure modes, PEP inference hallucination and template-regurgitation hallucination, operate through different mechanisms, concentrate in different model families, and would be entirely missed by the most common evaluation approaches. Claude Opus 4.7 avoided both failure modes entirely, demonstrating that the task is solvable without hallucination at the current frontier of model capability.

Organisations evaluating LLMs for adverse media screening should test against the specific entity categories most likely to trigger hallucination: PEP-adjacent entities, common-name entities, and entities with ambiguous or incomplete adverse media signals. Aggregate accuracy on clean entities is a necessary but far from sufficient condition for safe compliance deployment.
