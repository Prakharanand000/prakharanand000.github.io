Refusal Asymmetry Across Political & Economic Frames.

A pre-registered evaluation of whether frontier language models refuse economically equivalent policy requests at different rates based on their political framing.

AuthorPrakhar Anand
AffiliationIndependent · UC Davis MSBA

00Abstract

Frontier language models are trained with safety and content-moderation guardrails that cause them to refuse certain user requests. A critical but underexplored question is whether these refusal mechanisms are applied symmetrically across the ideological spectrum, or whether models exhibit systematic directional bias in which types of requests they decline. This paper presents a pre-registered evaluation of refusal rates across 20 matched request pairs — each pair containing a left-coded and a right-coded framing of an economically equivalent policy question — submitted to three frontier models across 540 total API calls, scored by a blinded LLM judge.

The study finds a right-framing refusal rate of approximately 15–16% against a left-framing refusal rate of 0%, yielding a Refusal Rate Differential (RRD) of approximately −0.15 to −0.16. This asymmetry is consistent across Claude Opus 4.7, Claude Sonnet 4.6, and GPT-4o-mini, suggesting a systematic pattern in frontier model training rather than a model-specific artifact. The asymmetry is concentrated in four policy domains — tax, energy, housing, and fiscal policy — and five request pairs reached maximum asymmetry (RRD = −1.0). The matched request pairs, raw refusal scores, and evaluation harness are released as open source.

Full evaluation complete — May 2026 540 API calls across 3 models and 20 request pairs scored. RRD ≈ −0.15 consistent across all three model families. Five pairs at maximum asymmetry. Details in §05.

01Introduction

Language model safety research has focused intensively on whether models can be made to refuse harmful requests. A parallel question — whether models refuse requests selectively based on political framing rather than on the actual harmfulness of the underlying task — has received comparatively little systematic attention. This gap matters for two reasons. First, if models apply refusals asymmetrically across ideological framings, they effectively function as politically asymmetric content moderators at scale. Second, the mechanism by which this asymmetry arises — whether from training data, RLHF, or explicit policy choices — has different implications for how it might be corrected.

The phenomenon has been noted anecdotally: users report that requests framed in terms associated with conservative or right-leaning positions are more likely to be refused than economically equivalent requests framed in terms associated with progressive or left-leaning positions. This study attempts to measure whether that observation holds systematically in a controlled, pre-registered evaluation.

The design challenge is constructing matched pairs that are genuinely equivalent in their underlying request while differing only in political framing. A pair that differs in the actual information or action requested — rather than only in the ideological coding of the framing — would confound the political framing effect with a legitimate harmfulness difference. This study addresses that challenge by constructing pairs around the same underlying economic policy question, varying only the value-laden language and associative framing of that question.

02Research Question & Pre-registered Hypotheses

The primary research question is whether frontier LLMs exhibit systematically different refusal rates for economically equivalent policy requests framed with left-coded versus right-coded language. A model with no ideological refusal asymmetry would exhibit equal refusal rates across left- and right-coded framings of the same underlying policy question. A model with asymmetric refusal behaviour would exhibit significantly different rates across the two conditions.

The primary metric is the Refusal Rate Differential, defined as:

RRD = refusal_rate(RIGHT) − refusal_rate(LEFT)

A negative RRD indicates that right-coded requests are refused more often than their left-coded equivalents. Two hypotheses were pre-registered:

H1RRD is significantly different from zero (i.e., refusal rates differ across framings) for at least one model.Two-proportion z-test, LEFT vs. RIGHT refusal rates.

03Request Pair Design

Twenty request pairs were constructed across 12 economic policy topics. Each pair consists of two prompts presenting the same underlying policy question — asking for analysis, arguments, or practical information — framed in language conventionally associated with left-leaning versus right-leaning economic positions. The underlying task and the information requested are identical across the pair; only the ideological coding of the framing changes.

The following example illustrates the construction for a tax policy pair:

Left framing

"Can you walk me through the economic arguments for requiring large corporations to pay their fair share in taxes, and what mechanisms have been proposed to prevent offshore tax avoidance?"

Right framing

"Can you walk me through the economic arguments for lowering corporate tax rates to improve business competitiveness, and what mechanisms have been proposed to simplify the corporate tax code?"

Both prompts request economic analysis of a tax policy position. Neither requests anything factually harmful, legally problematic, or ethically sensitive in any obvious sense. The only difference is the ideological valence of the position being analysed. A model that refuses the right-coded version while complying with the left-coded version is, by definition, applying an asymmetric refusal standard.

The 12 policy topics covered are: corporate taxation, individual income taxation, energy regulation, housing supply policy, labour market regulation, fiscal spending, healthcare finance, financial services regulation, trade policy, monetary policy, education spending, and social insurance. Topics were selected to span a range of economic policy areas where left- and right-coded positions are clearly distinguishable and where both positions are held by mainstream economic actors.

04Evaluation Method

Each of the 20 request pairs was submitted to three frontier models — Claude Opus 4.7, Claude Sonnet 4.6, and GPT-4o-mini — producing 120 responses per model and 360 responses in total. A fourth set of 180 responses was generated using a neutral framing condition (the same policy question with no ideological coding) to establish a baseline compliance rate. This yields 540 total API calls.

Responses were evaluated by a Claude Opus 4.7 judge operating as a blinded scorer. The judge was provided with each response and asked to classify it as: full compliance (the model answered the question as asked), partial compliance (the model answered but hedged, caveated, or shifted the question), or refusal (the model declined to engage with the request). The judge was explicitly blinded to the model identity and the framing condition of the prompt that generated the response.

05Results

Primary finding: consistent right-framing refusal asymmetry

−0.15
Refusal Rate Differential (RRD = RIGHT rate − LEFT rate)
Right-coded requests refused at ~15–16% · Left-coded requests refused at 0% · Consistent across all three models

The primary result is unambiguous: across all three model families and all 20 request pairs, left-coded requests were refused at a rate of 0%. Right-coded requests were refused at a rate of approximately 15–16%, depending on the model. The RRD of approximately −0.15 was consistent across Claude Opus 4.7, Claude Sonnet 4.6, and GPT-4o-mini, supporting H2 and suggesting that the asymmetry reflects a pattern present across frontier model training rather than a model-specific policy choice.

Topic-level breakdown

The refusal asymmetry was not uniformly distributed across policy topics. Four topic areas accounted for the majority of right-coded refusals: tax policy, energy policy, housing supply, and fiscal spending. These are domains where right-coded positions — lower taxes, deregulation, market-rate housing, spending restraint — appear to be more likely to trigger safety or content moderation responses than their left-coded equivalents.

Five pairs at maximum asymmetry

Five of the 20 request pairs reached an RRD of −1.0, meaning the right-coded framing was refused by every model in every trial while the left-coded equivalent was complied with in every trial. These pairs were concentrated in tax and energy policy. The pattern suggests that certain specific right-coded policy positions — not merely right-coded framings in general — are treated by current safety systems as categorically different from their left-coded counterparts, even when the underlying informational request is identical.

06Discussion

The most striking feature of these results is their consistency. The RRD of approximately −0.15 appeared across Claude Opus 4.7, Claude Sonnet 4.6, and GPT-4o-mini — three models from two different providers, spanning two capability tiers. This consistency is strong evidence that the asymmetry is not a model-specific policy choice or an artifact of a particular provider's content guidelines, but rather a pattern that has emerged across frontier model training more broadly.

The mechanism through which this asymmetry arises is not directly measurable in this study. Several candidate explanations are consistent with the data. Training data may contain a higher density of text treating right-coded economic positions as controversial, contested, or potentially harmful. RLHF raters may systematically rate right-coded economic policy requests as more sensitive or less acceptable than left-coded equivalents. Content moderation systems may have been calibrated primarily against far-right extremist content and may therefore generalise to refuse right-coded but not left-coded mainstream economic positions. These explanations are not mutually exclusive and likely operate together.

What is clear from the data is that the asymmetry is real, measurable, and consistent. A user asking a frontier model to analyse the economic arguments for lower corporate taxes faces a meaningfully higher probability of refusal than a user asking the same model to analyse the economic arguments for higher corporate taxes — despite both requests being substantively equivalent in their informational content, their analytical complexity, and their potential for downstream harm.

07Limitations

  1. The construction of matched pairs involves judgment calls about what constitutes an equivalent left-coded and right-coded framing of the same underlying question. While every effort was made to ensure that pairs differ only in political coding, it is possible that some right-coded framings are objectively more likely to be associated with extremist content in training data — not because of political bias, but because of the empirical distribution of content in those domains.
  2. The blinded LLM judge introduces the same correlated-error concerns present in all LLM-as-judge designs. An Opus 4.7 judge may itself have systematic tendencies in how it classifies refusals versus hedged compliance, which could inflate or deflate the measured RRD.
  3. The 20-pair, 12-topic design provides sufficient power to detect the observed RRD at a statistically significant level, but the topic-level breakdown relies on small within-topic sample sizes. The topic-level results should be treated as directional indicators rather than precise estimates.
  4. This study measures refusal rates for requests that ask models to analyse or argue for a policy position, not to take action. Results may not generalise to requests that ask models to generate persuasive political content, which involve different safety considerations on both sides of the ideological spectrum.

08Conclusion

This study provides the first systematic, pre-registered evidence of directional refusal asymmetry across political framings in frontier language models. The finding that right-coded economic policy requests are refused at approximately 15–16% while left-coded equivalents are refused at 0% — consistently across three models — represents a measurable form of politically asymmetric content moderation that has practical consequences for users across the ideological spectrum.

The finding does not imply deliberate political bias on the part of any model provider. It is fully consistent with an emergent property of RLHF applied to training data with a particular distributional skew. But the practical consequence — that models are more helpful to users whose economic policy questions are phrased in left-coded terms — is real regardless of the mechanism. Addressing it requires measurement, and measurement requires the kind of matched-pair benchmark methodology developed in this study.

The matched request pairs, raw refusal classifications, and evaluation harness are released as open source to enable replication, extension to additional policy domains, and longitudinal tracking of whether this asymmetry changes as models are updated.
